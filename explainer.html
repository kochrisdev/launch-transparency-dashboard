PART 1

Why this exists

For everyone — no prior knowledge needed.

In one sentence

New malaria medicines take 10–15 years to travel from "approved" to actually reaching patients, largely because no one can see where each medicine is stuck — so LAUNCH built a public dashboard that shows every medicine, every gate it must pass, and every bottleneck, with a dated reason on each delay.

The problem: approval is not access

Malaria still kills on the order of half a million people a year, and progress has stalled since 2015. New medicines exist, or are close — yet historically it has taken 10 to 15 years or more for a malaria product to move from regulatory approval to routine use in the countries that need it. One worked example from this project's own documentation: pyrethroid-PBO bednets took eight years just to move from an interim to a full WHO recommendation — eight years spent inside a single gate, after the science was settled.

The clock now matters more than ever. Artemisinin partial resistance — malaria parasites responding more slowly to the core drug class behind nearly all modern treatment — has emerged in Southeast Asia and is confirmed in parts of East Africa. Medicines that work against resistant parasites are in the pipeline, but a decade-long lag in adoption could arrive too late.

The diagnosis: a coordination failure

The delay is largely a visibility problem, not a science problem. A medicine's journey to patients runs through many independent institutions — the WHO, regulators, funders, manufacturers, and health ministries — each waiting on signals from the others. Nobody can see the whole board, so delays are discovered years after they begin.

LAUNCH (the Launch Transparency Initiative for new malaria tools, funded by Unitaid) is a bet that making the whole board visible changes behavior: if every actor can see every product, every gate, and every bottleneck — with a dated, written reason on each delay — partners can act on delays instead of discovering them later. The dashboard is that board. It will be hosted by the RBM Partnership to End Malaria, the global coordination platform for malaria, with technical oversight from the LAUNCH AMDR Core Advisory Group.

Three ideas that carry everything else

  1. "Approved" is not one fact — it is at least three. The WHO recommends a medicine, the WHO separately prequalifies its quality, and each country registers it — three independent decisions by three different bodies on three different calendars. Then a ministry must write it into national guidelines, a funder must buy it, and a supply chain must deliver it. Any of these gates can stall for years while all the others show green.
  2. The dashboard is read by the institutions it measures. WHO committees, funders, and manufacturers all see their own gates on the board. Every design and governance decision follows from that: a red light is an accusation in this domain, so the system makes an unsubstantiated accusation structurally impossible.
  3. Credibility comes from refusing to guess. Where a figure is unverified, the dashboard shows TBC ("to be confirmed") rather than an estimate. Every displayed number carries its public source and the date it was last verified. One invented number, discovered by the institutions being measured, would end the dashboard's credibility; an honest gap does not.

PART 2

The medicine's journey

For everyone — the domain the dashboard describes.

In one sentence

Every medicine on the board travels the same eight-stage pathway — one science stage, four separate approvals run by four different bodies, and a three-stage delivery chain — and the dashboard tracks which gate each medicine is at and where it is stuck.

The eight stages, in plain words

#StageWhat it meansWho runs it
0R&D & clinicalLaboratory development and human trials (Phase I safety → Phase II dosing → Phase III large multi-country efficacy trials, typically 3–6 years).Manufacturer + product development partnerships
1Regulatory approval (SRA)Review by a stringent regulator such as the EMA or US FDA. Malaria products usually use the EMA's EU-M4all procedure (formerly "Article 58") — a full EMA scientific review for medicines used outside the EU. This opinion anchors everything downstream.EMA / FDA
2WHO guidelinesWHO's expert group (the Guidelines Development Group) weighs the clinical evidence and decides whether WHO recommends the medicine. Countries largely copy these recommendations.WHO Global Malaria Programme
3WHO prequalificationWHO's quality check — dossier review plus factory inspections — that makes a product eligible for purchase by UN agencies and major donors. This is the ticket that lets the Global Fund buy it.WHO Prequalification Team
4Country registrationEach country's own regulator licenses the product nationally — often sequentially, country by country. The African Medicines Agency and WHO's collaborative registration procedure exist to speed this up.National regulatory authorities
5National policy adoptionThe ministry of health writes the medicine into national treatment guidelines — the document that determines what health workers actually prescribe and what the public sector buys. A WHO recommendation does not flow into these automatically.Ministries of health / NMCPs
6ProcurementSomeone actually pays: Global Fund grants (the majority of treatment volume), the US President's Malaria Initiative, UNICEF, domestic budgets. Tenders, reference pricing, demand forecasting.Global Fund, PMI, governments
7In-country deliveryThe last mile: supply chain from ports to clinics, health-worker training, job aids, and safety monitoring (pharmacovigilance).Governments + implementing partners

The traffic lights

Each medicine gets one light per stage — always paired with a symbol so the board works for colorblind readers and in print:

LightMeaning
✓ CompleteThis gate has been passed (with a cited source).
› In progressActively moving at a normal pace.
! DelayedStuck or slipped — a one-sentence, dated reason under the row says why. A red light can never appear without a written reason; the data validator makes that structurally impossible.
Not startedNot yet reached (dashed outline).

A halo ring marks the medicine's current position — the gate it is actively working through. Elapsed time between gates is colored on one scale everywhere: green ≤ 2 years, amber 3–5, red over 5.

The medicines tracked

LAUNCH deliberately picked two pairs that stress opposite halves of the pathway. The pipeline pair tests whether transparency speeds the regulatory half; the underutilized pair — fully approved for a decade yet barely used — shows what the adoption half costs when nobody is watching. On the board, the pipeline products light up red on the left half, the marketed products on the right half.

Pipeline · new chemical class

GanLum

ganaplacide–lumefantrine (KLU156) · Novartis + MMV

The first non-artemisinin novel-class combination in ~25 years — strategically huge because it works against artemisinin-resistant parasites. Its Phase III trial (KALUMA) met its endpoint in Nov 2025 with a 97.4% cure rate. The watch: how fast SRA → guidelines → prequalification goes.

Pipeline · triple ACT

ALAQ

artemether–lumefantrine–amodiaquine · Fosun Pharma + MORU

A triple ACT: a second partner drug added to today's standard treatment to protect it from resistance. Developed under the MORU-led DeTACT partnership, targeting approval and prequalification around 2027.

Marketed · underutilized

ASPY (Pyramax)

pyronaridine–artesunate · Shin Poong + MMV

EMA opinion 2012, prequalified, WHO-recommended 2022, registered in 25+ countries — yet in only a handful of national treatment guidelines. Its bottleneck is stage 5, policy adoption. The ten-year gap between EMA opinion and WHO recommendation is exactly the kind of interval the dashboard's timing chart exposes.

Marketed · underutilized

DHA–PPQ

dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine · Alfasigma, Guilin & others

Approved 2011, WHO-recommended 2015, multiple prequalified suppliers (which is supply security) — yet a small share of global treatment procurement. Its bottleneck is stage 6, procurement. Current use concentrates in seasonal prevention niches.

Future · prevention tool

Spatial emanators

airborne mosquito-repellent devices · SC Johnson

A placeholder row, pending funder approval (Gates Foundation). A prevention product travels a different WHO track (PQ Vector Control, a different guideline group), so the treatment stage names above won't literally apply — a known design decision waiting on activation. Two SC Johnson emanators were WHO PQ-listed in Aug 2025, so a real gate has already been passed.

Who's who

One institution deserves special care: the WHO wears three separate hats in this story — recommender (its Guidelines Development Group, stage 2), quality assessor (its Prequalification Team, stage 3), and overall coordinator. The project documentation is emphatic: never conflate them. The other actors:

  • EMA / FDA — stringent regulators; "SRA approval" in the data almost always means the EMA's EU-M4all opinion.
  • National regulators — one per country (stage 4); the African Medicines Agency and WHO's ~90-working-day collaborative registration procedure exist to accelerate them.
  • Ministries of health / national malaria control programmes — own national treatment guidelines (stage 5).
  • Global Fund, US PMI, UNICEF, domestic budgets — the buyers (stage 6). The Global Fund's public PQR database is the record of prices and volumes.
  • Product development partnerships — MMV (the dominant malaria PDP; co-developer of GanLum and ASPY), MORU (runs the DeTACT triple-ACT programme behind ALAQ), DNDi.
  • Manufacturers — Novartis, Fosun Pharma, Shin Poong, Alfasigma/Guilin, SC Johnson.
  • Unitaid — funds LAUNCH itself; RBM Partnership to End Malaria — will host the dashboard; the LAUNCH team supplies the analyst judgement behind each product's current position and bottleneck sentence.
PART 3

Reading the dashboard

For dashboard users — donors, ministries, implementing partners, manufacturers.

In one sentence

One row is one medicine's whole life story left-to-right; the red sentence under a row is the single most important thing on the page; and three trust signals — the banner, TBC, and the source lines — tell you exactly how much to believe every number.

The views

The same data file powers several presentations, each for a different reading style. Two page designs are currently live in parallel for a client decision; one will be retained.

ViewPageWhat it's for
Journey boardindex.htmlDesign Option A (default). One row per medicine, its full pathway left to right. Emphasises each product's story. Has the country map, timing chart, CSV download, glossary tooltips, print view, and the Recent updates panel.
Comparison matrixoption-b.htmlDesign Option B. Stages as rows, medicines as columns — emphasises cross-product comparison at each gate. Same data, distinct visual identity.
Pipeline posterpipeline.htmlThe whole portfolio laid out MMV-poster style, by development phase (Preclinical → Approved & scaling up), with a prevention lane. Complementary — not part of the A/B decision.
Data storystory.html"The Waiting Years" — a scroll-driven narrative for donors and advocacy: one medicine's decade from approval to still-pending adoption. Every number is computed live from the data, so the story updates itself.
Widgetwidget.htmlAn embeddable one-row tracker for partner sites (?product=aspy etc.) — an iframe snippet drops a live single-medicine tracker into any web page.

Three parallel editions of all views also exist: a Unitaid-branded edition (brand review), a synthetic-data edition (entirely fictional data for safe development — marked with an unmissable purple warning strip), and a temporary v2 preview showing proposed data updates awaiting LAUNCH sign-off.

How to read a row

  • Start with the red sentence. The one-line note under a row names the bottleneck LAUNCH is tracking for that product, with a date. It is the initiative's core editorial output — the central UI element, not a decoration.
  • The halo ring marks where the product is right now.
  • Hover any stage dot for status detail, the next expected step, and the date the information was last verified.
  • Click a row for the full profile: target use case, price, access and supply commitments, adoption requirements, country access counts, operational research, procured volumes by channel, the time-between-gates panel, and the full milestone table — the accountability ledger, where every row carries its own source.
  • Dotted-underlined terms (SRA, PQ, MFT…) show plain-language definitions on hover or keyboard focus; a Definitions panel at the bottom lists them all.
  • Returning visitors: start at Recent updates (bottom of page) — what changed and when. An RSS feed (feed.xml) carries every change.

The three trust signals

  1. The banner under the title. Design mockup = placeholder layout data. Draft data = compiled from public sources, pending verification. No banner = verified, live data. (The whole board is currently in draft.)
  2. TBC. The LAUNCH team does not yet have a verifiable figure. The dashboard never estimates: an honest gap beats an invented number. Most visible on price.
  3. Provenance lines under figures — the public source, whether the manufacturer confirmed release in writing, and the date last verified.

Two rules worth knowing the reasoning behind:

  • The price rule. A price appears only when the figure is public or the manufacturer has confirmed in writing that it may be shared; until then it reads TBC. Manufacturers operate tiered, confidential pricing — publishing an unconfirmed number could damage the very relationships LAUNCH needs.
  • The map rule. The country access map shades each country by highest level achieved (registered → in national guidelines → in MFT plans). While the underlying country survey is unverified it carries an ILLUSTRATIVE warning and must not be cited — a colored country reads as a fact in a way a TBC count never does, so the map gets a stricter verified-only standard.

Who uses it, and how

AudienceTypical use
Donors & fundersSee which products are stuck at procurement; read procured volumes by channel; export CSV for their own reporting.
Ministries of healthCheck country access status, the adoption-requirements panel (what a country must do before use — training, guideline updates, safety monitoring), and peer-country status on the map.
Implementing partnersTrack operational research (who is studying real-world use, where, until when) and the delivery stage.
ManufacturersSee their product's board position, confirm or correct the price field, and see where downstream gates stand.
Anyone republishingEverything shown is public — use Download CSV and cite the dashboard and the underlying sources; or embed the per-medicine widget.

Update cadence: milestones are reviewed monthly and updated within days of major public events; procurement and country data quarterly. The "last updated" date sits top right. Errors can be reported to the LAUNCH team (via RBM once hosted there) or as a GitHub issue; corrections are logged in Recent updates.

PART 4

Where the numbers come from

For analysts and anyone who needs to trust (or maintain) the data.

In one sentence

Robots collect evidence from public sources into a staging area, a human analyst decides what becomes dashboard data, and an automated validator — the only gate — makes it structurally impossible for the board to show an unsourced price, an unexplained delay, or a silently lying traffic light.

One file, one gate

All routine updates change exactly one file: data/products.js — a single, human-readable data file holding every product, stage status, milestone, price, and country status, plus a glossary and a changelog. Every view, edition, and export reads from it. Before any change ships, a validator script checks it against the governance rules; the validator's own stated purpose is that "a traffic light must never be able to lie silently."

Every value carries its provenance

  • source — a public, citable origin (WHO PQ list, EMA register, press release).
  • asOf — the date the value was last verified.
  • confirmedInWriting — the manufacturer confirmed release of this figure in writing. The register of those written confirmations deliberately lives outside the public repository.
  • TBC — honestly unknown. Never estimated.

The governance rules the validator enforces

RulePlain meaning
Red needs a reasonA delayed stage must carry a substantive written note, and the product must carry a top-level bottleneck sentence. An unsubstantiated accusation is structurally impossible.
Prices are earnedA displayed price requires written manufacturer confirmation or a public source. Otherwise it must read TBC.
No invented countsCountry counts must be real non-negative integers or TBC — "an invented number is worse than an admitted gap."
Volumes must add upProcurement channel splits must sum to 100%; a missing volume needs a written explanation of why.
Structure is sacredExactly 8 stage entries per product, valid status values, valid dates, unique permanent product IDs, chronological journey gates, one map entry per country.
Definitions are realGlossary definitions must be actual sentences; milestone completions should cite a source (warned if not).

Summary statistics (medicines tracked, active bottlenecks) are computed from the data at render time — they can never disagree with the board.

The analyst loop

  1. Edit data/products.js.
  2. Run the validator — not optional.
  3. Add a changelog entry and bump the "last updated" date.
  4. Commit and push — the live site updates within about two minutes.
  5. Automation does the rest: a dated snapshot of the data file is committed to history/ (the raw material for future trend charts), and the RSS feed is rebuilt.

A quarterly script also generates a "what changed" brief — stage movements versus the history snapshots, logged updates, active bottlenecks, and every remaining TBC gap as the verification to-do list. A GitHub issue opens automatically on the 1st of each month with the milestone-scan checklist.

The sourcing layer in more detail

CollectorSource & cadenceFeeds
fetch-trials.jsClinicalTrials.gov API v2 — weekly, automated. Diffs status, phase, completion-date and results changes; slipped dates are early bottleneck warnings.Stage 0 evidence, operational research, milestones
fetch-regulatory.jsWHO prequalification lists (medicines + vector control) and EMA EU-M4all opinions — monthly, automated. Watches for new listings, delistings, opinion changes.Stages 1, 3, 4
fetch-globalfund.jsGlobal Fund Data Service API (grants + disbursements) — monthly, automated.Funding-flow context for stage 6

Three rules keep this layer honest: raw snapshots are dated and append-only (so the past can be replayed and diffed); staging files are regenerated by script and never hand-edited; and every staging row carries its source URL and retrieval date, so provenance survives the trip into the dashboard.

Source hierarchy and the confirmation register

When sources disagree, analysts prefer, in order: WHO lists and guidelines → stringent regulators (EMA, FDA) → procurement databases (Global Fund PQR, PMI reports) → national regulators and ministries → manufacturer announcements (fine for milestones, never sufficient alone for prices) → LAUNCH's own surveys, labelled as draft until verified.

Written manufacturer confirmations (who confirmed what, when, under what conditions) are kept in a register outside the public repository — by policy, since they are relationship-sensitive. The public data file only ever carries the boolean flag.

PART 5

How it is built

For developers and technical reviewers.

In one sentence

A deliberately static, zero-build, zero-backend site — plain HTML pages rendering one strict-JSON data file with vanilla JavaScript, deployed by GitHub Pages on every push, with all automation (validation, snapshots, feeds, scheduled fetches) living in four GitHub Actions workflows.

Why so deliberately simple

The dataset is tiny (a few products × ~10 metrics), the maintainers are analysts rather than developers, and the 18-month initiative will be handed over to another organisation — so there must be nothing to install, upgrade, or break. There is no framework, no bundler, no package.json at the repo root. The repository root is the website.

The single data contract is a JavaScript file that assigns strict JSON to window.LAUNCH_DATA. Using a script assignment instead of fetching a .json file is deliberate: it works when an analyst double-clicks index.html from their file system — no server, no CORS. The body must stay strict JSON because the validator (and every other consumer, from the RSS builder to Power BI's query) extracts and parses it with the same idiom.

How a page renders

Each page is self-contained: design tokens and components in a <style> block, an HTML skeleton, and one render script that reads the data object and builds the DOM — deriving the summary stats from the data (so they can never disagree with the board), drawing the stage tracks, wiring tooltips and keyboard access, painting the country map from generated Natural Earth geometry, and positioning the timing chart with plain percent-positioned elements (no chart library). Every interpolated data value passes through an escaping function; status is always shape + color, never color alone; both light and dark themes are handled at the token level.

Generated editions — regenerate, don't hand-edit

Three builder scripts each read the original pages and write a themed or re-datasourced copy into a committed folder: unitaid/ (brand-token override appended as the last stylesheet, so the skin can't drift from the source), synthetic/ (points at the fictional dataset, adds an unmissable purple warning strip), and v2/ (points at the proposal dataset, adds a preview strip; the whole edition is deleted once the data is signed off and merged). The iron rule for all committed build output — editions, map geometry, history snapshots, feeds, briefs, CSV exports: rerun the builder, never edit the output.

The four CI workflows

WorkflowTriggerWhat it does
ValidateEvery push and pull requestRuns the validator against all three datasets (real, v2, synthetic) and builds the single-file preview as a smoke test. The quality gate.
Snapshot & feedPush to main touching data/products.js onlyValidates, copies the data file to a dated history/ snapshot (append-only — refuses to overwrite a same-day snapshot with different content), rebuilds feed.xml, and bot-commits. Its own commit can't retrigger it.
Monthly reminder1st of each monthOpens a GitHub issue with the milestone-scan checklist. Deliberately a reminder, not a scraper — most watched sources are CMS pages that change cosmetically, and hash-watching them would cry wolf; only stable-API sources are automated.
Scheduled source fetchMondays (trials); 3rd of month (Global Fund + regulatory); on demandRuns the fetchers, commits only under sourcing/ (which the snapshot workflow ignores, so a fetch can never trigger a dashboard change), and opens a watch issue when a report contains changes.

GitHub Pages deploys the repo root on every push to main — live in one to two minutes. CI validates on the same push but does not gate the deploy: a red validation run is a fix-now / revert-now signal.

The parallel platforms

  • Streamlit app (streamlit-app/) — a Python twin of all views, used as the analyst workbench: it accepts the bundled file, a URL, or a dragged-in draft data file, and shows live governance-check results — so an analyst can preview and validate a data update before committing it. Also offers runtime configuration the static site can't: product filters, adjustable timing thresholds, and a "hide unverified map" toggle.
  • Power BI kit (powerbi/) — not a binary report but a kit: Power Query scripts that load eight relational tables live from the published site (so reports refresh with the data), offline CSV exports of the same shape, a theme, DAX measures (including ones that carry the governance rules — the data-status banner and the unverified-map warning travel into Power BI too), and a page-by-page build guide.

Handover

Three options for RBM, all cheap because the site is static files: copy the file set to any path on their own site; keep the Pages deployment (or a fork under an RBM GitHub org) and embed it in an iframe; or transfer the repository — history and CI move with it, only the URL changes (two hardcoded URL constants and partner embed snippets need updating).

PART 6

Glossary

Plain-language definitions of every recurring term.

ACT
Artemisinin-based combination therapy — a fast-acting artemisinin drug plus a longer-acting partner drug; the global standard treatment for uncomplicated malaria. One product (artemether–lumefantrine) is ~70%+ of the market; everything on this board is trying to diversify away from that monoculture.
TACT / triple ACT
An ACT with a second partner drug added to protect it from resistance (ALAQ is one).
Artemisinin partial resistance
Parasites cleared more slowly by the core drug class — not full treatment failure yet in Africa, but the urgency driver behind everything here.
SRA
Stringent regulatory authority — an advanced regulator such as the EMA or US FDA.
EU-M4all / Article 58
The EMA procedure giving a full scientific review to medicines intended for use outside the EU — the usual first approval for malaria medicines.
Prequalification (PQ)
WHO's quality and manufacturing assessment (dossier + factory inspections) that makes a product eligible for purchase by UN agencies and major donors. A quality judgement, not an efficacy one.
EOI
Expression of Interest — the WHO list a product must be invited onto before it can be prequalified.
GDG
Guidelines Development Group — the WHO expert committee that decides recommendations, meeting on its own calendar (a classic delay hotspot).
NMCP
National malaria control programme — the ministry unit that owns a country's malaria strategy and treatment guidelines.
MFT
Multiple first-line therapies — deploying two or three different first-line treatments at once (by district or age band) to spread drug pressure and slow resistance; the main use case under which new products enter.
PDP
Product development partnership — a non-profit that co-develops medicines with industry (MMV, DNDi, MORU's DeTACT programme).
PQR
The Global Fund's public Price & Quality Reporting database — the public record of what was bought, in what volume, at what price.
CRP / AMA
WHO's collaborative registration procedure (~90 working days, reusing SRA/PQ assessments) and the African Medicines Agency — the two accelerators for country-by-country registration.
Pharmacovigilance
Ongoing monitoring of a medicine's safety in real-world use — a standard adoption requirement.
Operational research
Real-world implementation studies — distinct from the clinical trials that got the product approved.
"Treatment" as a unit
One full course for one patient. Procured volumes count courses, not tablets or packs.
TBC
To be confirmed — the LAUNCH team does not yet have a verifiable figure, and the dashboard never estimates.
Bottleneck flag
The one-sentence, dated reason a product is stuck — the initiative's core editorial output, structurally required wherever a red light appears.

Current status & open items

An honest snapshot, so no reader over-trusts the prototype.

  • The data is in draft. Compiled from public sources, pending LAUNCH-team verification and written manufacturer confirmations. Every page says so in its banner.
  • Two page designs are live in parallel (journey board vs comparison matrix) awaiting a client decision; the loser will be deleted and its extras folded into the winner.
  • The country map shows an ILLUSTRATIVE warning until the country survey is verified.
  • The timing thresholds (≤2 / 3–5 / >5 years) are acknowledged draft placeholders pending LAUNCH's benchmark work.
  • A v2 data preview is awaiting sign-off — the first harvest of collected public-source data (procurement volumes, regulatory dates, trial records) proposed as updates, kept separate until approved.
  • Hosting, branding, and analytics are pending external parties: RBM's web team (hosting model), RBM brand guidelines (a one-token-block rebrand), and a usage-analytics decision.
